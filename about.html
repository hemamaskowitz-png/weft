About

Weaving the fabric of time

Weft sets the events of world history side by side so you can see what usually gets told separately: how a coup becomes a novel, how a famine becomes a song, and who was standing where when it happened.

warp — the lanes, running through time weft — the threads woven across them

Why “Weft”

On a loom, the warp threads run lengthwise and hold everything in place. The weft is the thread passed across them, over and under, and it’s the weft that makes the pattern.

Here the warp is time, split into lanes: politics, science, music, sport, religion, fashion, disasters and the rest. Each event is a knot on its lane. The threads are the weft. They cross from lane to lane to show what led to what: a war and the photograph that turned opinion against it, an earthquake and the book of survivors’ voices that followed.

History is usually taught one strand at a time. Weft is an attempt to see the cloth.

Art as a response

The spark for Weft was a simple question: what did people make in answer to what was happening around them? Paintings, songs, protest posters, guerrilla installations, novels, memoirs, reportage.

Those works sit in Weft beside the events that prompted them. They carry a white ring, and each card says what the work was “made in response to”, or what art an event went on to inspire. Where a work came first and the event followed, the two are joined by an ordinary thread instead.

People and places

The who in history matters as much as the when and why. Every event names the people involved and where it happened. Tap a person to see when and where they were born and died, and to follow their lifeline through the events they appear in. Tap a place to find it on the globe and see what else happened there.

Regions

You can look at Weft one part of the world at a time. Choose a region, and its events stay bright while the rest of the world fades back. The threads that lead out of it stay visible, because no region’s history happened on its own.

  • Whose mapWeft groups places using the United Nations’ standard regions, known as M49: five continents divided into 22 sub-regions. The UN says these groupings are for statistical convenience and imply no political position, and Weft uses them in the same spirit.
  • The namesWeft says “Western Asia” and “Eastern Asia” rather than “Middle East” and “Far East”. The older names describe places by their distance from Europe; the newer ones describe where they are.
  • Today’s bordersPlaces are filed under the countries that exist now, so Waterloo is in Belgium and 19th-century Basutoland is in Lesotho. Some placements follow the UN rather than habit: Iran is in Southern Asia, Egypt and Sudan are in Northern Africa, and Türkiye and Cyprus are in Western Asia. Hawaii is counted with the United States, though events from the time of the Hawaiian Kingdom also appear under Polynesia.
  • More than one placeSome events belong to more than one region — indentured labourers sailing from India to Natal, say — and appear under each. Events at sea or in space belong to none.
  • Not a quotaWeft doesn’t try to give every region the same number of events. Some places and decades were quieter than others, and the powers of the day really did dominate the record. The aim is that nothing important is left out. If you find a gap, please tell us.

How to read it

The loom

The whole cloth

All of time as one loom of stars: today at the front, the past stretching away behind. Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom, and use the sliders to move through time.

2D timeline

A year at a time

Every lane laid out across the months. Step year by year, or switch to a single strip on smaller screens.

Globe

Where it happened

The same threads drawn as glowing arcs across a world map, lighting up as time passes.

Regions

One part of the world

Pick a continent or region from the menu at the top. Its events stay lit, everything else fades, and the choice follows you between the loom, the globe and the 2D timeline.

Search

Find anything

Type a person, place or event, or a date: “1984”, “Sep 1973”, “11 September 1973”, “1985–1989”, or mix them: “Chile 1973”.

Notes and disclaimers

  • AccuracyWeft is made with care, but it’s a simplified picture of a complicated past, and mistakes are possible. Summaries are short by design, and history is often contested. Treat Weft as a starting point and check anything important against primary sources.
  • DatesWhere only the year or month of something is known, Weft says so on the card rather than inventing a day. Those items sit mid-year or mid-month on the timeline.
  • Difficult subjectsHistory includes war, genocide, violence and death, and Weft doesn’t leave them out. Some cards and linked pictures may be distressing.
  • Artworks and booksWeft describes and links to works of art and literature; it doesn’t reproduce them. Copyright in those works stays with their artists, authors and estates.
  • Links to other sitesWeft links to Wikipedia and other websites it doesn’t control, and isn’t responsible for their content.
  • CorrectionsSpotted a mistake, or something important that’s missing? Corrections are welcome: report an error, or use the “Report an error” link on any card.

Privacy

Weft has no accounts, sets no cookies and runs no tracking or analytics. It doesn’t collect anything about you.

To work, your browser does talk to a few other services, and each of them will see your IP address like any website you visit:
